## Background

I had the opportunity to interview some candidates for an Exploratory Testing role recently within a team working on
data migration and orchestration for distributed analytics environments.  Seems strange to say, jobs in tech are
few and far between nowadays, and testing is often the first role to be seen as surplus to requirements in the
engineering process.

For years, I've been advocating for the importance of exploratory testing in software development. Specifically,
it's crucial in the type of software being developed at my current company. There are a few reasons for this
advocacy. The first is the complexity of the software and the environments it operates in. Distributed environments
(like Hadoop) have a huge number of moving parts, and customers utilise the components of the cluster in wildly
different ways. The environment you operate in for a financial customer is leaps and bounds from a retail customer.
This produces a large amount of integration issues that are not easily caught by automated tests. The second reason
is the need for a human touch. When you work in distributed environments, the number of customers you're considering
is small. There are fewer Hadoop clusters in the world than there are mobile phones, for example. Each cluster is
unique, and the requirements of each customer are different. This means that you need to have a deep understanding
of the problem the software is actually solving for the specific customer you're working with. This level of
flexibility and subjectivity is not something that can be automated. The third reason is the benefit that comes from
creativity. Exploratory testing is inherently a creative process. As the tester explores the software, they discover
areas of risk and they chase them. They can sniff out poor quality, and they can chase it to a set of improvements.
A strong exploratory tester can be a huge asset, they focus on important areas of risk and hone in on issues that
are not easily caught otherwise.

The challenge for hiring for this role is that it's not a role that you can expect prior experience in. It is an
unreasonable expectation to look for a candidate to come walking off the street with experience in Hadoop clusters.
There are very few people in the world with that experience, and they often are not looking for a job in exploratory
testing. The other issue is that exploratory testing is not a role that exists in many places. It's not a role that
is widely understood or appreciated. This means that you can't predict the range of candidates that you will get for
the role. It's very easy to hire the wrong person for the role.

With those challenges, I needed an approach to interviewing candidates that would allow me to assess their skills
and mindset for exploratory testing. Software engineering has practical interviews that are based on coding
exercises, why doesn't exploratory testing have the same?

## The inspiration

The inspiration for the practical interview came from a pair of paragons in the field of exploratory testing, James
Bach and Michael Bolton. They have both written extensively about the importance of the field, and I'm really just
echoing that sentiment.

In a [YouTube video](https://youtu.be/oyb8pDHx2Ww?si=PLpNy021T19yOLWO), James Bach conducts a mock interview for an
exploratory testing role. In the video, he is doing a review of a candidate's testing for a trivial web application.
This is a great example, and I put it into practice for my interviews.

## The interview

Firstly, pick a piece of software to get the person to test. Ideally, you need something with a user
interface and a bunch of bugs. This isn't hard to whip up as a purpose built application, but there are plenty of
applications on the internet that contain code that shouldn't have seen the light of day. The important thing is
that you understand the software, the reason it exists and the problems it is trying to solve. This is important
because you aren't just looking for a candidate to find bugs, you're looking for them to explore based on the
software's purpose and risks to real users.

With the software picked, the task is trivially simple (by design). Point the candidate at the software and ask them
to exploratory test it. Give them a minimum amount of time, so they have a rough gauge on the scope of the task, but
no maximum time. The minimum sets their scope, the lack of maximum allows you to assess if they know when to stop.
Tell them that they should make a note in the report of any questions they have whilst testing, and that the
questions won't get answered, but that you want to see what they are asking.

The output of the testing is their report. You should give them absolutely no guidance on the format of the report,
and you should never ask them for a specific template. The report is what you are using to assess the tester, you
need it to be as much their own work as possible.

## The review

After the candidate has done their testing, you should have an email with their report. This is where you can really
get to know the candidate. Testers reveal a huge amount about the way they test and they way they think when you
give them such free-reign on a task.

I don't want to give too much away about what I look for in the report, but if you know the craft of exploratory
testing, you will know what differentiates a good report from a bad one. You can always spot the testers who think
of themselves as "manual" testers and the testers who think of themselves as "exploratory" testers.

## My experience

I've done this interview process a few times now, and I've found it to be a great benefit to the overall process.
Earlier, I mentioned the challenges in hiring for this team: lack of similar job postings and a lack of candidates
with technical experience. This interview process has allowed me to clearly assess candidates abilities to do
exploratory testing without needing to see them having done it in some other company. On the technical side, you get
a bit of insight into tools that they might build to help them with their testing, but you need to supplement this
process with a person-to-person conversation to really drill into that aspect. This is a helpful tool in the toolbox,
but not a silver bullet.

## Conclusion

Two things:
1. Hire for exploratory testing if what I'm saying resonates with challenges you have.
2. If you do hire for exploratory testing, you can and should have a practical interview for it.
